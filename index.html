The Business of Crossing

Somewhere between the Guatemala border and the Sonoran Desert, a freight train decides who lives and who doesn’t. Deterrence doesn’t stop migration. It just decides who profits from the danger, and who pays for it. This website highlights what that actually looks like in policy, numbers, and the lives of the people living it — and why I think the usual sensationalist framing misses the point.

Migration Is a Sustainability Issue

It's easy to treat the border as a story about security. It's actually a story about three forces colliding: a warming climate displacing people, economies that depend on the people who leave, and enforcement policy that shapes how dangerous the journey becomes. None of the three can be understood alone.

Climate

Environmental disasters and slow-onset change are already displacing people at scale.

Economics

Remittances make migration economically load-bearing for the countries people leave.

Enforcement

Deterrence policy decides which routes people take, and how deadly those routes are.

Concerted emissions action could cut this by roughly 80%.

Deterrence Policy Has a Body Count

686

migrant deaths and disappearances documented on the U.S.–Mexico border in 2022, the deadliest land migration route in the world.

Prevention Through Deterrence deliberately routes crossings through terrain built to hurt people. De León calls it outsourcing violence to nature.

IOM Missing Migrants Project, 2022 Americas data

Recorded desert-crossing deaths, 2022

More desert deaths were recorded on this one border route than in the entire Sahara.

The Economy Runs on the People Leaving

30%

of Honduras's entire GDP now comes from remittances: money sent home by people who already left.

That's more than six times the global average. Migration isn't a side effect of a struggling economy; it's become load-bearing for it.

TheGlobalEconomy.com / World Bank, 2024–25

Remittances as a share of GDP, 2025

Latin America & the Caribbean received an estimated $163 billion in remittances in 2024 alone.

Smugglers Aren't Who You Think

It’s easy to picture a smuggler as a cartel operative moving people for profit at any cost. The people actually guiding migrants north look nothing like that, and merging the two misses who’s making this journey possible, and who’s actually getting hurt by it.

01

The assumptionSmugglers are traffickers

Guía, not trafficker

A smuggler is hired and trusted by the people they move; a trafficker uses force or coercion. Media and policy routinely collapse this distinction — De León insists on it.

Compared onSmuggler (guía)Trafficker
ConsentHired and agreed to by the migrantTaken by force, threat, or deception
After arrivalThe relationship endsExploitation continues
BorderAlways involves crossing oneCan happen within one country

02

The assumptionThey’re cartel kingpins

Often former migrants themselves

Guides like Chino, Santos, and Papo fell into the work as broke teenagers riding the same freight trains as their clients, not cartel kingpins, but kids with few other options.

The assumptionArrest them and the smuggling stops

Arrests don't fix anything

De León calls it “a beast with many heads.” Removing one low-level guide just opens a vacancy; the economy survives constant turnover at the bottom.

Where We Could All Dig Deeper

These are the questions this presentation opens but doesn’t settle: branch lines off the route, worth following on your own.

Branch line 1 · Not yet explored

Legal alternatives to enforcement

We talk a lot about what deterrence gets wrong; we spend less time on what a workable legal pathway would actually require.

A question to start with

What would a legal pathway need to offer for someone to choose it over hiring a guía?

Branch line 2 · Not yet explored

Race inside the migrant trail

Garifuna and other Afro-Central American migrants face discrimination from fellow migrants, smugglers, and officials alike, a layer most border coverage skips.

A question to start with

How does the journey change for a Garifuna migrant, and why doesn’t border coverage talk about it?

Branch line 3 · Not yet explored

What happens after deportation

Multiple returns, cycles of re-migration, and the long-term toll on families rarely make it into the policy conversation.

A question to start with

What happens to a family when the same person is deported, returns, and is deported again?

Branch line 4 · Not yet explored

U.S. policy's role in creating the problem

Cold War military aid and 1990s gang deportations directly shaped the violence driving migration today, worth knowing before debating solutions.

A question to start with

If U.S. policy helped create the violence people are fleeing, what does the U.S. owe the people fleeing it?

Why Leave, and Why Come to the U.S.?

Neither side explains migration alone. People are pushed out by conditions at home and pulled in by real demand for their work. The route between exists because both ends need it.

Art Has Been Saying This for Decades

This section links seven real works done in honor of immigrants and immigration. They are linked rather than copied, so credit and rights stay with the people who made them.

Public art · performance

“Borrando la Frontera” (Erasing the Border) — Ana Teresa Fernández

Fernández emigrated from Mexico to the U.S. at age 11. In 2011 she painted sections of the border wall sky-blue at the same crossing her family once used — an immigrant artist working directly on the structure this synthesis argues outsources violence to nature.

See the work ↗ Poem

“Aubade with La Bestia” — Alfredo Aguilar

A poem written from on top of the freight train itself, the same train at the center of this synthesis's route map and De León's fieldwork.

Read the poem ↗ Art installation

Hostile Terrain 94

De León's own participatory exhibit: volunteers worldwide hand-write over 3,000 toe tags, one per documented Sonoran Desert death, and pin them to a wall-sized map by exact coordinates.

See the project ↗ Song · official video

“La Jaula de Oro” — Los Tigres del Norte, 1983

A classic corrido written by Enrique Franco: an undocumented father has money and safety in the U.S. but calls it a “golden cage.” His children have forgotten Mexico, and he can never go home.

Watch on YouTube ↗ Photography · project

Items left behind

De León's Undocumented Migration Project has spent years documenting backpacks, water bottles, and other belongings abandoned in the desert — physical evidence most coverage never stops to photograph.

Undocumented Migration Project ↗ Documentary · trailer

Border South (2019)

Directed by Raúl O. Paz-Pastrana, follows the same southern-Mexico migrant trail this synthesis draws on, including De León's own fieldwork, across a full-length documentary.

Watch the trailer ↗ Talk · watch
